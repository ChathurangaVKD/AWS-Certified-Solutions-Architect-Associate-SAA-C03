    Lesson 1 · 5 min

    Every AWS request answers four questions

    IAM is just a rulebook. Each time anyone calls AWS, AWS reads the rules and says yes or no. Nothing more.

    PrincipalWho?A user, a role, a service, an account.
    ActionDoes what?s3:GetObject, ec2:StartInstances
    ResourceOn which thing?One bucket, one table, or * (everything).
    ConditionWhen?Only with MFA, only from this IP, only in this Region.

    Who can do what on which resource under which conditions. Every policy you ever read is this one sentence.

    Authentication

    "Who are you?" Password, MFA, access keys, or temporary credentials prove identity.

    Authorization

    "Are you allowed?" Policies decide. This is where most exam questions live.

    The office building analogy

    In AWSIn an office building
    Root userThe building owner. Master key to every door. Keep it in a safe.
    IAM userAn employee with a permanent badge.
    IAM groupA department. Give rights to "Finance", everyone in Finance gets them.
    IAM roleA visitor badge. You borrow it, it expires, you give it back.
    Identity policyYour job description: what you may do.
    Resource policyA sign on a door: who may enter this room.
    Permissions boundaryA clearance ceiling for one person. You can't go above it, whatever your job says.
    SCPHead-office rule for the whole branch. Even the branch manager must obey.
    Golden rule. By default everything is denied. An Allow opens a door. A Deny anywhere slams it shut, and no Allow can reopen it.
    Lesson 2 · 7 min

    Four kinds of "who"

    Three of them hold permissions directly. The group is only a folder of users.

    Root user

    all-powerful

    The email + password used to create the account. IAM policies cannot restrict it (only an SCP can, in a member account).

    Use it only for
    • Closing the account, changing root email
    • Changing the support plan
    • Restoring access if admins locked themselves out
    Exam answer
    • Turn on MFA
    • Delete root access keys
    • Never use it for daily work

    IAM user

    long-term creds

    One person or app with permanent credentials: a console password and/or access keys (for CLI/SDK).

    Remember
    • Permanent keys can leak, so rotate them
    • For people today, AWS prefers IAM Identity Center
    • For apps on AWS, use a role instead

    IAM group

    just a folder

    A set of users. Attach a policy to the group and every member gets it.

    Exam traps
    • Groups can't contain other groups
    • A group can't sign in and has no credentials
    • A group can't be the Principal in a resource policy

    IAM role

    temporary creds

    Permissions with no password and no permanent keys. Someone assumes it and gets short-lived credentials from STS.

    Has two policies
    • Trust policy: who may assume me
    • Permissions policy: what I may do
    Used by
    • EC2, Lambda, ECS tasks
    • Users from other accounts
    • Federated (SSO) users
    Rule of thumb for the exam. If an answer stores access keys on a server, in code, or in user data, it is almost always wrong. The right answer is a role.

    Lesson 4 · 8 min
    
  
    Some policies give. Others only limit.
    
  
    This one idea solves most "which policy type?" questions.
    

  
    Only two types can GRANT access: identity-based and resource-based policies. Permissions boundaries, SCPs and session policies never grant anything. They only set a ceiling.
    

  
    
    
    GRANTS
    Identity-based policy
    Attached to a user, group or role. Says what that identity can do. No Principal field, because the principal is whoever it's attached to.
    AWS managed (made by AWS, e.g. ReadOnlyAccess) · Customer managed (yours, reusable, versioned) · Inline (glued to one identity, deleted with it)
    
    
    GRANTS
    Resource-based policy
    Attached to the resource. Has a Principal field: who may touch this thing. Lets other accounts in without assuming a role.
    S3 bucket policy · SQS queue policy · SNS topic policy · KMS key policy · Lambda resource policy
    
    
    GRANTS
    Role trust policy
    A resource-based policy on a role. Answers only one question: who may assume this role.
    "ec2.amazonaws.com may assume me" · "Account 444455556666 may assume me"
    
    
    LIMITS
    Permissions boundary
    A managed policy set on one user or role as its maximum. Effective rights = identity policy ∩ boundary.
    Exam keyword: "let developers create roles safely", "delegate admin without privilege escalation"
    
    
    LIMITS
    Service Control Policy (SCP)
    AWS Organizations. Attached to the org root, an OU or an account. Caps every identity in member accounts, including their root user.
    Does NOT affect the management account · does not affect service-linked roles · exam keyword: "all accounts", "even administrators"
    
    
    LIMITS
    Session policy
    Passed when you assume a role or federate. Narrows that one session only.
    Rare on the exam. Know that it exists.
    
  
    

  
    Special case: KMS key policy
    
  
    Every KMS key must have a key policy. IAM policies only work for a key if its key policy allows the account to use IAM (the default key policy does this). If the key policy doesn't trust the account, no IAM policy can help.

      Three lines to memorise. 1) Explicit Deny always wins. 2) Silence is a Deny (implicit deny). 3) Every limiting layer (SCP, boundary, session) must also allow.
      

  
      Cross-account is different
      
  
      
    
      A user in Account A wants something in Account B. Both sides must say yes.
      
    
      Account A: the user's identity policy must allow the action.
      Account B: the resource policy (or a role's trust policy) must allow Account A.
      
    
      One side alone is not enough.

      Roles: borrow permissions, give them back
      
  
      STS (Security Token Service) hands out temporary keys. Nothing permanent to leak.
      

  
        
    
      1. Ask. A user, app or service calls sts:AssumeRole on a role ARN.
        2. 
    
      2. Check the trust policy. Is this caller listed as allowed to assume the role?
        3. 
    
      3. Get temporary credentials. Access key + secret + session token. Expire after the session duration (default 1 hour, role max up to 12 hours).
        4. 
    
      4. Act. Calls now run with the role's permissions policy, not the caller's own.

      Where roles appear on the exam
      
  
      
    
      EC2 instance profile
      A box that carries a role onto an EC2 instance. The SDK picks up the credentials from instance metadata and they rotate on their own.
      
    
      Lambda execution role
      What your function may do (write logs, read DynamoDB). Each function has one.
      
    
      Cross-account role
      Role in Account B trusts Account A. A's users need sts:AssumeRole on that role. Then they "switch role".
      
    
      Third party + ExternalId
      For a vendor's account, add an sts:ExternalId condition to the trust policy. Prevents the "confused deputy" problem.
      
    
      IAM Identity Center
      Workforce single sign-on to many accounts. Connects to Active Directory, Okta, Entra ID. Users get roles via permission sets.
      
    
      Amazon Cognito
      For your app's end users (mobile, web). User pools = sign in. Identity pools = temporary AWS credentials.
      
    
      SAML 2.0 / OIDC federation
      Corporate IdP or web identity trusted directly by IAM. Users get AssumeRoleWithSAML / AssumeRoleWithWebIdentity.
      
    
      iam:PassRole
      To give a role to a service (launch EC2 with a role, create a Lambda), you need iam:PassRole on that role.
